The work I’m proudest of

Six comments, one root cause

A design review on 20 August produced six consecutive comments on the same prototype: what is 68/100, what does veto power mean, what is this number. The obvious fix was to add labels.

They weren’t six copy problems. They were one provenance problem: the interface was printing TypeScript fields straight to the screen, and nobody had written down what those fields meant or where they came from. Adding labels would have patched the symptom and let the next field repeat it.

So I wrote a field map instead — every number on screen traced to the mock field behind it, to the section of the investment constitution that defines the concept, and to whether the backend actually computes it yet.

Judgment

Five calls I’d defend

Most of these cost us a feature or a number. That was the point: a financial product earns trust by what it refuses to say.

  1. Withhold a confidence band under 20 samples

    The 80–100% band had nine verdicts. Showing a hit rate for nine would have been the most flattering number on the page, and the least earned. It reads “not enough” instead.

  2. Say nothing on illiquid tickers

    Some listed names are too thin for the quant model to be reliable. Rather than return a hedged verdict, NAVI has a screen that explains why it won’t answer, and offers to tell you when it can.

  3. Split short and long horizons

    The same ticker is often “don’t open a position” over 15–30 sessions and “keep holding” over 6–18 months. Averaging them into one verdict destroys the information. Two verdicts, always, even when they disagree.

  4. Give risk a veto, not a vote

    A risk score that gets averaged with four other scores isn’t a risk control. The risk analyst can block a verdict outright, and the thresholds come from the three onboarding questions rather than a default.

  5. Turn metering off for the beta

    The credit system was built and working. Nothing should stand between a first-time user and their first analysis while we’re still learning what they want, so I switched it off in the UI and kept the hook. It’s in the demo behind a toggle.

Decisions in the build
  • Light-first, because the audience checks prices in daylight on a phone
  • Personalisation is real state, not copy — one onboarding answer reorders the home screen
  • Coach marks appear once per screen and dismiss themselves
  • Reduced motion keeps the content and drops only the movement
  • Every refused or empty state is designed, not a blank panel
